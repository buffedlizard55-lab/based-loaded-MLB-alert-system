What this system tracks —
and how to check it.

This page exists so nothing here has to be taken on trust. Every requirement from the project brief is listed with where it is implemented and how it can be checked; every baseball claim is tied to an official rules source; every data claim is tied to the exact official MLB endpoint behind it. Nothing on this site accepts manual input — all state is read from the official public MLB StatsAPI.

1. The one situation

The monitor tracks exactly one situation and alerts on nothing else. 

TIED SCORE                    (official linescore runs: away === home)
+ ALL THREE BASES OCCUPIED     (official offense.first, .second, .third)
+ BOTTOM OF INNING 9 OR LATER  (no upper limit: 9th, 10th, 11th, 12th, …)
+ FEWER THAN THREE OUTS        (any count, any number of outs before the 3rd)
= ALERT

The watch window opens earlier than the alert: as soon as a tied game reaches the changeover into the bottom of the 9th (or any later inning), the game is listed as on watch with its live bases, outs and count, so the load is visible as it happens rather than only at the end. Any half-inning that can end the game with a walk-off is covered, without an upper inning bound.

Why this situation matters, in the rulebook's own words — with the bases full, the batter is forced to advance and so are all runners, so a walk, hit batter or any other forced advance can end the game:

“When the winning run is scored in the last half-inning of a regulation game, or in the last half of an extra inning, as the result of a base on balls, hit batter or any other play with the bases full which forces the batter and all other runners to advance without liability of being put out, the umpire shall not declare the game ended until the runner forced to advance from third has touched home base and the batter-runner has touched first base.” Official Baseball Rules, Rule 5.08(b). Sources: 2023 Official Baseball Rules (PDF, MLB) · Rule 5.08 text listing (both checked 2026-09-29)

In extra innings the offense also starts each half-inning with a runner already on second base — one occupied base, not three, so it never alerts on its own:

“Amended Rule 7.01(b) to incorporate the parameters of the Extra Innings Rule, which includes starting each half-inning following the ninth inning with a runner on second base.” 2023 OBR, summary of rule changes, Rule 7.01(b). Official Baseball Rules 2023 (PDF, MLB), checked 2026-09-29

3. Every route to loaded bases

There is deliberately no list of play names to match. Each route below ends with the same thing — official occupancy on first, second and third — and the monitor reads that occupancy from the coherent linescore. That is why a route that has never been seen before cannot be missed, and why a play description can never trigger an alert by itself.

Route Official reference How the monitor handles it
Base hit / any fair ball by the batter Rule 5.05(a): “The batter becomes a runner when: (1) He hits a fair ball” Uniformly: read offense.first, offense.second, offense.third from the official live linescore. No route needs a special case; no event text is matched; a play that leaves all three bases occupied alerts exactly like any other.
Walk — four balls Rule 5.05(b)(1): “Four ‘balls’ have been called by the umpire”
Intentional walk (awarded on the manager's signal) Rule 5.05(b)(1) Comment: “including an award of first base to a batter by an umpire following a signal from a manager”
Hit by pitch Rule 5.05(b)(2): “He is touched by a pitched ball which he is not attempting to hit”
Catcher's or fielder's interference Rule 5.05(b)(3): “The catcher or any fielder interferes with him”
Uncaught third strike (first base open, or two out) Rule 5.05(a)(2): “The third strike called by the umpire is not caught, providing (1) first base is unoccupied, or (2) first base is occupied with two out”
Error, fielder's choice or force out replacing a runner Scoring under Rule 9.00; the bases on the linescore are the official record of who reached
Balk — every runner advances one base Rule 6.02(a): “A balk is committed; runners advance”
Obstruction — the umpire awards the base(s) Rule 6.01(h) (obstruction)
Wild pitch, passed ball, steal of a base Rule 9.13 (wild pitches and passed balls), Rule 9.07 (stolen bases and caught stealing)
Automatic extra-inning runner on second Rule 7.01(b) (Extra Innings Rule)
Replay review or an official scorer correction MLB instant-replay FAQ (reviewable-plays list)

Runners are only added by a batter becoming a runner or by an umpire's award. Wild pitches, passed balls, balks and steals move runners who are already on base — they cannot increase the number of occupied bases, so they can never be the play that loads them on their own. Rule numbers and text checked 2026-09-29 against the 2023 Official Baseball Rules (PDF), the Rule 5.0 text mirror on UmpireBible (5.01–5.06 read directly: 5.05(a)–(b) is the batter-becomes-a-runner rule cited above) and the Rule 5.08 text listing.

6. Limitations and the next sessions' work

Stated plainly, because a monitor that overstates itself is worse than no monitor. This section is reviewed together with README.md → “Where this still needs work” every session.

  • Browser-bound by default. The page alerts only while it is open and visible, which is the single biggest gap for daily use — but it is no longer the only way to watch. tools/watcher.mjs runs the same rules engine (assets/js/bases-loaded-core.js, required verbatim) outside the browser: same discovery cadence, same target rule, same situation logic, a JSONL log where every record carries its official snapshot URL, and optional phone push through a webhook, ntfy, or Web Push — the watcher now encrypts each alert to the subscribed device (RFC 8291) and signs it with its own VAPID key (RFC 8292), so the push service only ever holds ciphertext. Subscribe a device from the Phone alerts panel on the monitor. What it still needs is somewhere to run — an always-on machine or scheduler you provide; nothing is hosted for you.
  • Polling gaps. A situation that appears and resolves inside one gap — 2 s on the monitor, up to 5 s on the strip — can be missed, and upstream publication delays can hide a pitch or two. Nothing is back-filled.
  • History is per browser. Records live in this browser's localStorage (7 days, 200 records). They are not cross-device and are not a complete replay of the season.
  • Unofficial data source. The MLB StatsAPI has no SLA and no published rate limit; the client self-limits and degrades visibly instead of guessing.
  • Live end-to-end proof is still pending. The rule engine is covered by 11,772 deterministic checks, the monitor by 99, the strip by 160, the always-on watcher by 159, its deployment recipes by 112, the Web Push implementation by 130 (the RFC 8291 and RFC 8292 test vectors, intermediate values included), the phone-alerts panel by 74 and the static site by 347 checks (counts as of 2026-09-29), and the published copy of this site is verified by tools/deployed-site-test.mjs on every merge and nightly — but the honest next acceptance test is a real tied game entering the bottom of the 9th with the bases loaded, observed end to end, and carried all the way to a real phone notification.
  • Development-sandbox network limit (flagged, open). The sandbox used for this session cannot reach statsapi.mlb.com (TLS egress is blocked), so the API projections listed in §4 are carried forward from the last verified session and must be re-confirmed from a networked machine or the CI smoke workflow.
  • Recommended next work: (1) prove it live — one real tied game entering the bottom of the 9th with the bases loaded, watched by the running watcher with a subscribed phone, is the only acceptance test left; (2) a cross-device store for alert history, which the watcher's JSONL log is the natural beginning of; (3) an installable PWA manifest with icons (iOS Web Push already requires Add to Home Screen) and vibration; (4) watcher operations — a --doctor mode, retry/backoff for push 429/5xx, and CLI listing/pruning of stored subscriptions. Done already, and listed here so the list stays honest: the always-on watcher, its deployment recipes, exported history, the official per-alert citation link, and browser-grade Web Push — see the log below.

The site now carries a web-app manifest and generated icons, so it can be added to a home screen — which is what iOS requires before it will deliver Web Push at all — and notifications vibrate with a distinct two-short-one-long pattern shared by the monitor, the strip and the service worker. The icons are not artwork of unknown origin: tools/make-icons.mjs rasterises the infield diamond with all three bases from the site's own CSS tokens using only arithmetic and node:zlib, and tools/icons-test.mjs — 153 installability checks — decodes every committed PNG (signature, per-chunk CRC-32, inflated scanlines), re-renders it and requires the pixels to match, checks the maskable variants keep their art inside the safe zone, and fails if the service worker's buzz and the rules engine's buzz disagree.
  • Shipped Always-on watching without a browser (the number one limitation above). tools/watcher.mjs is one dependency-free file that requires the site's rules engine verbatim, polls the same official endpoints with the same cadences and the same target rule, keeps its de-duplication state on disk so a restart never re-alerts, appends every alert to a JSONL log where each record carries the exact statsapi.mlb.com snapshot URL it was read from, and delivers through a webhook or ntfy when configured. It reports a failed channel with its reason — never as delivered — and it never guesses from play text or event names. Covered by tools/watcher-test.mjs (159 checks) in CI, including a full encrypt → decrypt round trip of the bytes it posts for a notification, the capped retry/backoff for transient push failures, and the --doctor readiness report. Hosting it is a recipe now, not a research project: deploy/ ships a hardened systemd unit, a Dockerfile and compose file (unprivileged user, read-only root, volume-backed state) and a launchd plist, walked through step by step in docs/watcher-deployment.md, with a container healthcheck that reads the watcher's own state file and says plainly what it does not prove. What no recipe can supply is the machine it runs on.
  • Shipped Browser-grade Web Push (the limitation this list used to lead with). The watcher encrypts each alert to the subscribed device with tools/webpush.mjs — RFC 8291 (aes128gcm, HKDF over the ECDH secret) and RFC 8292 (VAPID, an ES256 JWT in the raw r||s form JOSE requires) implemented against Node's own crypto, no dependencies — so the push service transports ciphertext it cannot read. It is verified against the RFCs' published vectors value by value (including the ECDH secret, PRK_key, IKM, PRK, CEK and nonce) by tools/webpush-test.mjs (130 checks), and the watcher test decrypts the bytes it actually posts with an independently written receiver path. Two things the tests caught: the RFC's own Content-Length: 145 disagrees with its decoded 144-octet body (the bytes win, and the discrepancy is documented where a reader hits it), and the 3993-octet plaintext limit is asserted as arithmetic — 4096 − 86 header − 1 delimiter − 16 tag — so it cannot quietly drift. Delivery is reported per device (push(phone): HTTP 201), a subscription the service reports gone is dropped from the store, and endpoints are masked in logs. The monitor's Phone alerts panel subscribes a device and hands over the store entry (74 checks); it states plainly that a static site cannot reach your watcher, so the entry travels by copy/paste.
  • Shipped Alert history is now exportable (JSON and CSV) and citable: every exported record carries the exact official snapshot URL it was read from (statsapi.mlb.com/api/v1.1/game/{gamePk}/feed/live), each history card links to that same snapshot, and one click copies a single-line evidence citation. Because this history lives in one browser, the shared link points at the official record — never at a private local list. The serializers are pure functions in bases-loaded-core.js and are covered by the rule suite (CSV quoting, RFC 4180 line endings, JSON schema, the evidence line, file naming).
  • Corrected The deployment check failed its first run on main for a timing reason, not a content one: GitHub Pages builds after the merge commit, so the check was comparing against the previous deployment. It now retries the whole assessment — 30 rounds × 15 s in CI — until the published bytes match, and its annotations name the differing byte and point at the Pages build when a mismatch persists.
